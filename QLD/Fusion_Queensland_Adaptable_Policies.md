## Make Public Transport Accessible

Public transport accessibility obligations would link a published register of verified barriers to a funded remediation program, with responsible asset owners, delivery dates and independent compliance checks covering complete passenger journeys.

### The Problem

An accessible vehicle is not enough if a passenger cannot reach the stop, board safely, change services or obtain usable information. Queensland should organise upgrades around complete journeys and the barriers identified by passengers.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost surveys, design, construction, replacement transport and ongoing maintenance separately. Coordinate with asset renewal, but disclose additional spending and displaced projects. No statewide dollar estimate is asserted.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Transport capital and accessibility programs, with maintenance and renewal budgets. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Visible barriers** — Publish an asset-level register of verified barriers, ownership, assessment dates, funding status and planned remedies.
- **Complete journeys** — Prioritise continuous accessible journeys to health care, education, employment and essential services, with disability organisations helping set priorities.
- **Accessible facilities** — Include paths, crossings, lighting, boarding areas, lifts, toilets where relevant, hearing support and readable passenger information in project scopes.
- **Replacement travel** — Require accessible replacement services and clear advance advice during planned closures; publish arrangements for unplanned disruption.
- **Funded repairs** — Fund repeatable small works in a rolling program and independently check completed upgrades with users before closing each register entry.

### Research behind this policy

Translink publishes accessibility information and a Disability Action Plan.

**Design Rationale**

A journey can fail at a path, boarding point, interchange or passenger-information interface even when the vehicle is accessible. Asset-level reporting needs to distinguish an isolated improvement from a continuous usable journey. Compliance assessment must therefore use the applicable legal standards rather than treating a completed construction project as proof of accessibility.

**System Interaction**

Responsibility follows asset ownership; a council path and an operator vehicle may form parts of the same journey without having the same owner. Compliance records need assessment dates and a clear distinction between verified barriers and unassessed assets. Disability organisations can test practical usability without replacing formal legal assessment. Temporary closures require accessible alternatives within the same journey obligation.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Accessibility investment has both capital and maintenance costs. Coordinating it with asset renewal can avoid repeated works, but does not remove the cost of making an entire journey usable.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Co-design with people with varied disabilities. Maintain assistance during works. Record exemptions and unresolved barriers openly without publishing personal passenger information.

Years 2-4: expand the program, report quarterly and independently audit completed journeys. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Require Backup Plans for Transport Failures

Critical transport services would be subject to independently tested resilience requirements covering essential dependencies, recovery arrangements and accessible replacement services, with non-sensitive findings published.

### The Problem

Network resilience requires more than an extra route on a map. Communications, power, control systems, staffing and accessible replacement capacity all matter. Queensland should test vulnerabilities before deciding which investments produce the greatest reduction in disruption.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Appraise audits, backup systems, drills, reserve capacity and infrastructure separately. Compare expected disruption reductions with lifecycle costs. No universal redundancy mandate or savings figure is assumed.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Transport operations and capital programs, subject to a resilience investment appraisal. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Critical dependencies** — Audit critical dependencies across communications, power, control centres, interchange access and constrained transport corridors.
- **Resilience requirements** — Require proportionate resilience plans for major new projects and significant contract renewals, including independently tested recovery procedures.
- **Replacement capacity** — Arrange accessible replacement capacity, staff mobilisation and passenger information protocols for high-impact disruption scenarios.
- **Proportionate investment** — Evaluate alternative route connections and operational workarounds before committing to expensive duplicate infrastructure.
- **Public findings** — Publish non-sensitive audit findings and annual recovery exercises, with secure handling of exploitable system details.

### Research behind this policy

Translink publishes schedule and real-time data that can support service monitoring. These feeds do not themselves establish the resilience of Queensland infrastructure.

[Translink open data: schedules and operational feeds](https://translink.com.au/about-translink/open-data)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Communications, power and operational dependencies can transmit a local failure across a wider service. Resilience requirements need to distinguish safe recovery from unrestricted continued operation. Duplicate infrastructure is not the only response: assessed fallback arrangements may address a vulnerability without replicating an entire corridor.

**System Interaction**

The requirements apply to dependencies capable of materially interrupting essential services. Rail safety remains a binding constraint on recovery options. Public accountability can disclose assessment coverage and recovery performance while withholding exploitable security details. A tested fallback can still offer lower capacity than normal operation; that capacity difference is a residual service trade-off even where the arrangements operate as designed.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Backup arrangements have an ongoing carrying cost even when rarely used. Investment appraisal must compare recovery capacity with the service disruption it addresses.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Rail safety remains paramount: a fallback is not permission to operate unsafely. Publish accountability without exposing cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Include regional and disabled passengers in exercises.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Translink publishes schedule and real-time data that can support service monitoring. These feeds do not themselves establish the resilience of Queensland infrastructure. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Request incident logs, dependency inventories, safety advice, replacement fleet capacity and credible recovery costs.

**Implementation Outline**

First 6 months: assign TMR coordination and commission an independent critical-dependency review. Months 6-18: fund priority operational fixes and conduct joint operator recovery exercises. Years 2-4: incorporate justified capital works into investment plans and retest failures after upgrades. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Set Standards for On-Demand Transport

State-supported on-demand transport contracts would specify booking access, pickup performance, service hours, accessible travel and interchange obligations, with published performance reporting and evaluation before renewal or expansion.

### The Problem

Flexible transport should provide a usable connection rather than an uncertain booking. Standards must reflect local geography, disability access and operating costs. Fixed routes should remain the preferred option where demand supports reliable frequent service.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Model vehicles, drivers, dispatch, accessible capacity, dead running and minimum contract payments. Report the additional subsidy explicitly; fares alone should not be assumed to cover delivery.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Public transport operating contracts and any approved local trial allocation. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Clear service information** — Publish booking methods, service hours, eligibility, pickup arrangements, cancellation rules and what happens after a missed connection.
- **Booking and pickup standards** — Set contract-level booking and pickup standards following local trials, reporting median and 90th-percentile waits rather than averages alone.
- **Accessible booking** — Provide telephone booking and accessible vehicles; offer door-to-door assistance where assessed access needs justify it.
- **Connected journeys** — Integrate fares, passenger information and timed interchange arrangements with the wider network wherever feasible.
- **Compare service models** — Compare fixed-route, flexible-route and community-transport options before extending a pilot or replacing an existing service.

### Research behind this policy

Translink operates a Queensland network with published schedule information. The performance of each on-demand service needs a separate local assessment; Victorian booking experiences are not Queensland evidence.

[Translink open data: schedules and operational feeds](https://translink.com.au/about-translink/open-data)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

An on-demand label does not identify the service actually available. Booking channels, geographic coverage and pickup arrangements define who can use it. Evaluation must compare equivalent travel needs rather than treating low expenditure per booking as sufficient evidence of useful access.

**System Interaction**

Contract standards distinguish ordinary zone-based travel from assistance justified by a passenger's assessed accessibility needs. Telephone access is part of booking access, not an optional separate digital-inclusion program. Fixed routes and flexible services remain alternative operating models. Variable demand and long travel distances can leave a capacity-versus-waiting-time trade-off even when an operator meets its contractual obligations.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Demand-responsive services face vehicle, driver and dispatch costs even when bookings are sparse. Cost per journey must be considered alongside whether essential travel is actually available.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Avoid app-only access. Protect booking histories. Do not remove fixed services until a replacement passes a published access test and transition support is available.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Translink operates a Queensland network with published schedule information. The performance of each on-demand service needs a separate local assessment; Victorian booking experiences are not Queensland evidence. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Obtain contract service standards, rejected-booking data, accessibility demand and comparative fixed-route costs.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: audit existing contracts, users, unmet requests and waiting times. Months 6-18: trial revised standards in contrasting urban and regional settings. Years 2-4: scale only services meeting access, reliability and cost tests; redesign or stop unsuccessful pilots. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Coordinate Public Transport Timetables

Public transport timetable planning would require shared-corridor spacing and interchange checks, with feasible changes incorporated into operator schedules and assessed against actual running times.

### The Problem

Two buses arriving together can leave a long gap even where a corridor has several routes. Better coordination may improve usefulness, but changes must account for school travel, driver duties, congestion and connections elsewhere in the network.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost planning staff, consultation, systems changes and any additional operating hours. Distinguish changes possible within existing resources from changes requiring extra vehicles or staffing.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Transport planning and operating budgets, with additional service hours separately approved. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Audit connections** — Audit shared corridors, transfer waits and gaps across peaks, off-peak hours, evenings and weekends.
- **Coordinate departures** — Stagger compatible routes where feasible and coordinate bus, rail, tram and ferry connections around passenger needs.
- **Protect transfer access** — Protect useful timed connections on lower-frequency routes and allow realistic transfer times for disabled passengers.
- **Test changes** — Test changes with operational data and passenger consultation, publishing trade-offs where improving one connection worsens another.
- **Publish timetable updates** — Provide an open change log and update passenger information before each timetable change.

### Research behind this policy

Translink publishes machine-readable timetables and selected real-time feeds. These allow a Queensland audit; this draft does not assert a measured number of avoidable timetable clashes.

[Translink open data: schedules and operational feeds](https://translink.com.au/about-translink/open-data)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Service counts can overstate practical frequency when compatible routes arrive together. Conversely, deliberate alignment may serve a transfer or school journey. Timetable coordination therefore needs a network-level view of passenger connections rather than an assumption that every simultaneous arrival is an error.

**System Interaction**

Running-time variability and driver-duty constraints limit feasible spacing. Improving one interchange can worsen another because a vehicle cannot satisfy every connection simultaneously. This remains a genuine trade-off under correctly coordinated schedules. Scheduled waiting times and observed waiting times measure different things and should not be presented as interchangeable evidence.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Coordinating existing departures can improve their usefulness without increasing the number of services. Vehicle availability, staffing and running times determine whether a change is achievable within current resources.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Keep safe transfer allowances and driver fatigue protections. Publish changes affecting school or low-frequency travel. Do not claim timetable optimisation is automatically cost-free.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Translink publishes machine-readable timetables and selected real-time feeds. These allow a Queensland audit; this draft does not assert a measured number of avoidable timetable clashes. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Confirm route calendars, observed running times, duty constraints and which connections passengers actually use.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: build the corridor audit from current GTFS and validate against operations. Months 6-18: trial priority timetable changes and measure transfer and waiting outcomes. Years 2-4: embed coordination checks in routine timetable and contract planning. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Expand Frequent Transport in Underserved Areas

Public transport service allocation would use published access assessments to direct staged operating investment to underserved corridors, combining frequent fixed routes with connecting services and locally evaluated alternatives.

### The Problem

A low fare has limited value without a service that runs when people need it. Queensland should rank underserved communities by population, essential destinations and disadvantage, then fund practical connections instead of assuming every place needs the same mode.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Publish recurring driver, fleet, fuel or energy and maintenance costs as well as stop and priority works. Secure operating funding before promising a network. No route or farebox revenue assumption substitutes for a business case.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Additional public transport operating allocations and supporting capital programs. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Map service gaps** — Publish maps of service frequency, operating hours and realistic walking access, with missing-data areas clearly identified.
- **Rank improvements** — Rank improvements using resident need, disadvantage, growth, essential destinations and achievable network benefits.
- **Connect trunk routes and feeders** — Develop frequent trunk buses with dependable feeders and safe interchange access; appraise bus priority where delays undermine the service.
- **Use suitable regional models** — Use regional service designs suited to local demand, including evaluated flexible options where frequent fixed routes are unsuitable.
- **Publish funded service plans** — Publish corridor service plans, recurrent funding and outcomes, separating current commitments from proposed expansions.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland timetable data supports assessment of scheduled services. A timetable score does not measure actual punctuality; Queensland baselines must be built and labelled accordingly.

[Translink open data: schedules and operational feeds](https://translink.com.au/about-translink/open-data)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

An affordable fare does not resolve a missing service or a service operating outside useful hours. Access assessment needs the location of residents, essential destinations and actual walking connections. A single urban frequency threshold is not an adequate description of need across all regional settings.

**System Interaction**

Timetable data measure promised service, while observed reliability requires separate evidence. Areas lacking usable data must be distinguished from areas verified to lack service. Patronage is relevant but cannot alone capture essential access for small or disadvantaged populations. Demand responses and development timing remain uncertain even with a functioning allocation process, so access outcomes and operating costs require joint evaluation.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Frequent service requires recurrent operating funding, not only new stops or vehicles. Low patronage can reflect poor access as well as low demand, so allocation needs both access and cost evidence.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Include shift workers, carers, young people and disabled passengers. Do not equate unavailable data with zero service or rank sparsely populated communities only by patronage.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland timetable data supports assessment of scheduled services. A timetable score does not measure actual punctuality; Queensland baselines must be built and labelled accordingly. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Confirm current timetables, population distribution, walkable paths, fleet availability and route-level operating costs.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: establish the Queensland access baseline and identify candidate corridors. Months 6-18: consult communities and fund staged service increases with operational capacity secured. Years 2-4: expand successful corridors, redesign weak services and refresh access maps. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Bring Services into New Neighbourhoods

Major growth-area planning would require costed service-sequencing agreements that connect housing occupation with transport, utility and essential local-service delivery, naming responsible authorities and lawful funding obligations.

### The Problem

Housing delivery and service delivery should be planned together. Sequencing transport, utilities, safe paths and community facilities can reduce dependence on long car trips, while flexible local land uses allow shops and services to emerge as communities grow.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost early services, coordination and infrastructure delivery. Allocate state, council and proponent responsibilities transparently, testing impacts on housing prices and project viability.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — State infrastructure and service budgets, council programs and lawful proponent contributions. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Sequence services** — Require a costed service-sequencing plan for major growth areas, naming responsible agencies, funding and staged delivery triggers.
- **Allow local uses** — Permit appropriate small shops, community uses and diverse housing through clear local standards and infrastructure capacity tests.
- **Connect new residents** — Coordinate initial public transport and safe walking links with occupation of major residential stages.
- **Publish capacity assumptions** — Publish school, health, water, sewer and community-facility capacity assumptions, with updates when development timing changes.
- **Fund early services** — Use early service funding and staged infrastructure agreements to close gaps without imposing conditions unrelated to a development.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland planning operates under its own state and local planning framework.

[Queensland planning framework](https://www.planning.qld.gov.au/planning-framework)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Residential development and public services have different funding and construction cycles. An approval may assume capacity or future services that are not yet funded. Sequencing agreements address the relationship between occupation and service readiness rather than treating every facility as a developer obligation.

**System Interaction**

State agencies, councils, utilities and proponents retain their lawful responsibilities. Agreements must identify both the planned service and the party responsible for funding it. Conditions cannot substitute for powers an authority does not hold. Development timing can change even where commitments are fulfilled, leaving a trade-off between early service provision and temporary underuse. This item changes sequencing; housing-permission rules remain a separate reform surface.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Service sequencing distributes costs between infrastructure owners and service providers. Earlier provision may require public spending before a growing neighbourhood produces its full revenue base.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Maintain flood, biodiversity and accessibility safeguards. Avoid blanket moratoria or retrospective conditions that delay viable homes. Consult Traditional Owners and local residents early.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland planning operates under its own state and local planning framework. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Obtain growth staging, utility capacity, service lead times, lawful contribution mechanisms and agency delivery commitments.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: review legal powers, existing infrastructure plans and priority growth-area gaps. Months 6-18: pilot coordinated service agreements with councils and state agencies. Years 2-4: incorporate evaluated requirements into relevant planning instruments and funding programs. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Allow More Homes Near Public Transport

Planning schemes would incorporate a Queensland housing code permitting diverse housing and compatible local uses in defined serviced transport precincts, with objective design, infrastructure and hazard requirements and explicit terms for any affordability-linked incentives.

### The Problem

Predictable planning can support duplexes, terraces, townhouses, small apartments and compatible local uses. The appropriate scale depends on infrastructure and actual access. More supply must be accompanied by a separate enforceable affordability mechanism where public benefits are offered.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost precinct utilities, public realm, staffing and transport services. Model incentive value and developer viability. Publish any public subsidy and foregone revenue separately.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Planning, transport and infrastructure budgets, with lawful contributions assessed precinct by precinct. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Set housing standards** — Develop a Queensland model code for diverse housing in suitable serviced areas, with clear design and infrastructure tests.
- **Map useful catchments** — Map precincts using actual walking connections and service quality, rather than one universal station radius.
- **Plan mixed precincts** — Coordinate homes, shops, employment, public space and accessible paths around major transport hubs.
- **Test affordability measures** — Test enforceable affordable-housing obligations or incentives, specifying eligibility, rent limits, duration and monitoring.
- **Publish infrastructure plans** — Publish precinct infrastructure capacity and delivery plans before granting additional development capacity.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland has its own planning framework. Station-area permissions and infrastructure responsibilities require review of state instruments and individual council schemes.

[Queensland planning framework](https://www.planning.qld.gov.au/planning-framework)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Permitted housing forms and feasible housing projects are not the same. Clear permissions can remove a legal barrier, but utilities, land assembly, construction and finance still determine delivery. Precinct boundaries need to reflect usable transport access and capacity rather than treating proximity alone as sufficient.

**System Interaction**

Objective requirements preserve mapped hazard and environmental constraints without allowing unrelated discretionary barriers to recreate the original restriction. Affordability-linked incentives need defined beneficiaries, rent or price treatment, duration and enforceability; extra density alone is not an affordability guarantee. This code changes permissions, not the separate service-sequencing agreements or public ownership of homes. Market financing constraints remain even where the code operates correctly.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Permission for more homes and delivery of affordable homes are different institutional results. Utility capacity, development viability and public subsidy need separate treatment.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Keep flood, heat, accessibility and ecological safeguards. Assess displacement and construction impacts. Additional density is not assumed to deliver affordability automatically.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland-specific evidence still required: Obtain site capacity, infrastructure costs, lawful planning mechanisms and feasibility evidence before setting numeric obligations.

Months 6-18: consult on model standards, appraise infrastructure and test affordability mechanisms. Years 2-4: adopt suitable instruments, monitor completions and revise standards where outcomes disappoint. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Build Permanent Public Homes Near Transport

Suitable publicly controlled transport land would be available for affordable rental housing under retained public ownership or enforceable community-housing arrangements, with transport reservations protected and long-term rent, eligibility and maintenance obligations attached to each project.

### The Problem

Well-located public land can support homes close to jobs and services. Land is not free: its alternative use and infrastructure needs must be valued. Each project should secure lasting affordability, fit operational requirements and have a credible long-term maintenance plan.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Model land opportunity cost, capital, utilities, finance, rent revenue and lifecycle maintenance. Report net subsidy and debt exposure. No zero-cost or self-funding claim is made.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — State social-housing capital and maintenance programs; any borrowing and rental income require a full financing model. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Protect transport needs** — Audit publicly controlled transport sites and identify land genuinely available after future rail, bus, interchange and access needs are protected.
- **Compare ownership models** — Compare retained public ownership, community-housing partnerships and other lawful delivery structures on whole-life public value.
- **Secure affordable rentals** — Specify affordable rental eligibility, rent-setting, duration and monitoring; retain public or community benefit through enforceable arrangements.
- **Design complete communities** — Integrate accessible homes, shops and community space with safe transport access and appropriate parking transition.
- **Publish delivery and costs** — Publish project costs, subsidy, delivery milestones, tenancy outcomes and maintenance performance.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland planning rules provide the local statutory setting.

[Queensland planning framework](https://www.planning.qld.gov.au/planning-framework)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Public transport land may have future operational uses, contamination or infrastructure constraints. Availability cannot be inferred from an apparently unused site. Affordable rental outcomes depend on durable tenancy and ownership arrangements rather than a temporary discount at initial occupation.

**System Interaction**

Transport reservations define the land available for housing before project appraisal. Retained ownership and enforceable provider agreements are alternative ways of preserving the stated public benefit. Rent treatment, eligibility, maintenance and restrictions on disposal must remain effective through changes in management. A viable project may still require an ongoing subsidy because land opportunity cost and lifecycle expenditure persist despite retained ownership.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Public housing creates a long-lived public asset and a continuing maintenance obligation. Rental receipts do not establish that construction, land and financing costs are fully recovered.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

## Keep Strategic Assets in Public Hands

Major decisions about strategic public assets would require a published public-interest appraisal before disposal, concession extension or acquisition, with retained public ownership as the default and any departure supported by a whole-life assessment.

### The Problem

Ownership decisions should be judged by service quality, resilience, affordability and long-term returns. Public ownership is a policy preference for strategic assets, but acquisition, contract change or operation must be assessed honestly rather than presented as a guaranteed saving.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Include valuation, compensation, transaction costs, workforce transition, capital renewal and operating liabilities. A proposal to retain income also has financing and opportunity costs.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Relevant asset-owner and public-sector governance budgets; retention or acquisition requires an approved financing plan. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Register strategic assets** — Create a public register of major strategic assets, ownership, concession expiry dates and material state commitments.
- **Appraise major decisions** — Require public-interest appraisals before major disposals, long concessions or contract extensions.
- **Compare operating models** — Compare continued ownership, public operation, regulated contracting and other feasible models using consistent whole-life assumptions.
- **Publish public-interest findings** — Publish reasons and non-sensitive appraisal findings, including compensation, transition risk and foregone income.
- **Fund operational capability** — Require a funded capability and maintenance plan whenever public operation or acquisition is proposed.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland publishes a government procurement framework. Each asset also has its own governance and contractual setting, which must be examined individually.

[Queensland government procurement framework](https://www.hpw.qld.gov.au/what-we-do/procurement)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

An upfront receipt is not equivalent to the long-term value of an asset. Ownership, service delivery and commercial risk can move separately under a concession. Comparison requires consistent treatment of retained income, capital renewal, financing and contingent liabilities.

**System Interaction**

Retained ownership is the default, not a claim that every acquisition is economical. Existing contractual rights remain relevant to compensation and timing. A public-interest appraisal tests feasible alternatives rather than comparing a fully costed private option against an uncosted public model. Valuation uncertainty and future demand remain live even with sound appraisal. This item governs ownership and concessions; transport operating-contract selection is distinct.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Retaining an income stream has financing and opportunity costs. A public ownership decision must compare long-run returns, liabilities and service obligations with the alternatives.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

### The Problem

Night-time safety depends on more than patrol numbers. Transport availability, lighting, crowd movement, venue practice, health response and targeted enforcement should work together. Each Queensland precinct needs its own evidence and delivery agreement.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost late-night service hours, public-space works, venue contributions, health support and policing separately. Explain state, council and private funding shares.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Transport, health, police and council precinct budgets, with any lawful venue contributions separately identified. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Coordinate precinct plans** — Develop precinct safety plans with councils, police, venue operators, health services, workers and residents.
- **Map transport and harm** — Map late-night transport gaps, accessible pickup points, pedestrian risks and recurrent harm locations.
- **Trial safe routes home** — Trial coordinated last-service times, safe waiting areas and clear information where demand and evidence justify them.
- **Combine local responses** — Combine venue compliance and proportionate policing with first aid, referral and crowd-management measures.
- **Evaluate nearby effects** — Evaluate effects on violence, injuries, harassment and nearby streets, publishing results without exposing victims.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland publishes crime information and transport schedules. Neither alone provides a complete picture of night-time harm, so health and precinct data must be sought.

[QPS information asset register and crime data](https://www.police.qld.gov.au/rights-information/information-asset-register) | 
[Translink open data: schedules and operational feeds](https://translink.com.au/about-translink/open-data)

<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Crowd movement, venue behaviour and last-service times interact within a night-time precinct. A measure aimed only at one component can move queues or harm to another. A precinct agreement treats these interactions as one local management surface rather than unrelated statewide promises.

**System Interaction**

Agreements retain the lawful responsibilities of police, councils, health services, transport authorities and venues. Incident and injury data need compatible geographic and time boundaries. Accessibility and residential amenity shape the options available. Greater late-night access can also alter demand and dispersal patterns; neighbouring-street impacts remain an evaluation issue even when the agreed measures operate correctly.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Night-time safety spans several budgets and private responsibilities. A precinct appraisal should identify who bears each cost and whether an intervention changes harm or moves it elsewhere.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Include women, disabled people, LGBTQIA+ communities and late-shift workers. Prevent exclusionary enforcement and protect residential amenity.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland publishes crime information and transport schedules. Neither alone provides a complete picture of night-time harm, so health and precinct data must be sought. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Seek precinct incident timing, injury data, staffing and transport costs; assess licensing and data-sharing powers before final commitments.

Months 6-18: deliver coordinated transport and safety trials. Years 2-4: expand effective measures and adapt unsuccessful precinct designs. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Expand Accountable Youth Diversion

Eligible youth-justice referrals would connect suitable diversion to available support and case follow-through, with risk assessment, voluntary victim participation in restorative processes and independent evaluation of reoffending and victim experience.

### The Problem

Young people need consistent pathways that address harm, responsibility and support needs. Queensland should improve access to suitable early intervention and evaluate diversion by outcomes, while maintaining an appropriate separate response to serious violence and high risk.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost specialist workers, service capacity, transport and evaluation. Potential detention or enforcement savings require evidence and should not fund promises in advance.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Youth-justice and relevant support-service allocations. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Earlier support** — Map unmet youth-support needs across school engagement, housing, family support, mental health and substance-use services.
- **Suitable diversion** — Improve access to suitable lawful diversion using clear eligibility, risk assessment and case coordination.
- **Victim choice** — Offer restorative processes only through lawful pathways, with informed voluntary victim participation and support.
- **Service follow-through** — Provide consistent case follow-through so referrals lead to available services rather than waiting lists alone.
- **Evaluate outcomes** — Independently evaluate reoffending, victim experience and wellbeing; publish de-identified results and unequal access.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland already provides youth restorative justice conferences.

[Queensland youth restorative justice conferencing](https://www.youthjustice.qld.gov.au/programs-initiatives/initiatives/restorativejustice/about)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

A referral is not equivalent to an available service. Queensland already has restorative justice pathways, so access and follow-through are the immediate design questions. Eligibility must distinguish suitable diversion from the response required for serious violence or high risk.

**System Interaction**

A victim's decision not to participate does not remove their entitlement to support. Child privacy and procedural rights remain boundaries on supervision and reporting. Diversion should not draw low-risk children into unnecessary justice involvement. Comparing reoffending requires attention to selection differences; apparently improved outcomes may reflect the characteristics of eligible participants even where the referral system functions correctly.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

A referral only becomes a service when suitable workers and support are available. Any claimed detention or enforcement savings require evidence rather than advance booking.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Protect child privacy and procedural rights. Victims may decline restorative participation. Avoid drawing low-risk young people unnecessarily into justice supervision.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland already provides youth restorative justice conferences. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Confirm current youth-justice provisions, diversion eligibility, comparative outcome data and provider capacity before setting expansion targets.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: review current law, eligibility, service capacity and unmet needs. Months 6-18: fund selected capacity gaps and evaluated local pilots. Years 2-4: improve effective pathways and revise programs that do not reduce harm. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Trial Neighbourhood Police Posts

Local police access would be trialled through staffed neighbourhood posts or scheduled outreach at selected activity centres, with published availability, protected emergency-response capacity and independent comparison against other local service models.

### The Problem

Small police posts and foot or bicycle patrols may improve access and local relationships. They should be tested against other uses of scarce officers, rather than assumed to deliver safety simply because a new shopfront opens.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost leases, fit-out, accessibility, officers, security and ongoing operation. Compare with mobile services and existing capacity. New premises do not create additional officers.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — Police workforce and facilities trial allocations. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Trial local access** — Trial staffed local posts or scheduled outreach at suitable transport hubs and activity centres.
- **Choose suitable sites** — Choose sites using community access, harm patterns, officer availability and comparative service options.
- **Publish availability** — Publish opening hours and purpose so residents understand when assistance is available and how to contact emergency services.
- **Connect local responses** — Pair local presence with lawful patrols, community problem-solving and reliable referral arrangements.
- **Compare service models** — Evaluate posts against mobile outreach and existing stations before permanent expansion.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland crime-data resources can support pilot selection, while the CCC provides existing police integrity oversight.

[QPS information asset register and crime data](https://www.police.qld.gov.au/rights-information/information-asset-register) | 
[CCC: Queensland police oversight](https://www.ccc.qld.gov.au/corruption/police-oversight)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

A shopfront changes the place police can be contacted, but does not create additional officers. Local access must be assessed against mobile outreach and existing stations. Crime and confidence are separate outcomes and neither should be inferred from the opening of premises alone.

**System Interaction**

Published availability defines the service residents can expect and distinguishes it from an emergency response facility. Confidential reporting needs privacy within the local premises. Emergency capacity constrains the trial design, but a remaining opportunity cost exists whenever officers are assigned to one form of presence rather than another. A site may improve access without demonstrating a measurable reduction in local crime.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

A local post changes how an existing workforce is deployed. Premises costs and officer time must be compared with mobile outreach and existing stations.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

No enforcement quotas or surveillance expansion by default. Provide private spaces for sensitive reports and maintain independent complaint access.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland crime-data resources can support pilot selection, while the CCC provides existing police integrity oversight. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Obtain workforce availability, local demand, response-time baselines, site safety and comparative operating costs.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: select trial sites and assess staffing and emergency-response impacts. Months 6-18: operate pilots with clear hours, safeguards and evaluation. Years 2-4: retain effective posts, relocate or close weak trials and publish the reasons. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

## Let Communities Decide Local Spending

Defined discretionary local investment budgets would be allocated through voluntary state-council participatory budgeting pilots, with published project rules, supported resident participation, conflict disclosure and decisions confined to lawful authority.

### The Problem

Participatory budgeting can make priorities visible and improve local ownership. Queensland should start with clearly funded pilots and defined decision boundaries, ensuring that organised groups do not crowd out residents who have less time or confidence to participate.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Publish the allocated project budget plus facilitation, outreach, feasibility and maintenance costs. Explain which discretionary spending is redirected and protect essential statutory services.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — A defined existing discretionary project budget, plus separately funded participation and administration. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Define the budget** — Create voluntary state-council pilots for a defined, published discretionary investment envelope.
- **Invite feasible projects** — Invite residents to propose projects and publish eligibility, costs, maintenance responsibilities and delivery constraints.
- **Support participation** — Provide supported participation through meetings, accessible digital options and outreach to underrepresented groups.
- **Transparent selection** — Use transparent selection rules, conflict declarations and independent facilitation; explain how decisions affect the budget.
- **Publish delivery and review** — Publish selected projects, rejected proposals with reasons, delivery progress and independent evaluation.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland's planning framework allocates formal responsibilities to public authorities. Community participation cannot silently replace statutory decision-making or elected accountability.

[Queensland planning framework](https://www.planning.qld.gov.au/planning-framework)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Consultation can invite preferences without identifying what residents are authorised to decide. A defined budget and project boundary make that authority intelligible. Participation must be assessed by who is reached, not only by the number of responses received.

**System Interaction**

The pilot envelope excludes decisions outside lawful delegation and does not transfer statutory planning approval powers. Project eligibility needs lifecycle maintenance responsibility, not only construction cost. Supported participation and conflicts management address access and capture within the same allocation process. Majority preference can still differ from minority needs even when participation is representative; statutory rights remain outside the spending vote.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Participatory budgeting changes who selects eligible projects within a defined envelope. Facilitation and maintenance costs are additional to the visible construction or purchase price.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Protect minority rights and avoid political capture. Do not delegate approvals beyond lawful powers. Account for people without digital access and those unable to attend meetings.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland's planning framework allocates formal responsibilities to public authorities. Community participation cannot silently replace statutory decision-making or elected accountability. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Determine lawful delegation, council agreement, eligible expenditure, maintenance liability and a feasible inclusive selection process.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: agree pilot partners, legal boundaries and a funded envelope. Months 6-18: run proposal development and selection with community support. Years 2-4: deliver selected projects, evaluate inclusion and consider expansion. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

**References**

[Queensland planning framework](https://www.planning.qld.gov.au/planning-framework)

## Bring Citizens into Major Policy Decisions

Selected major policy decisions would receive recommendations from independently administered citizens assemblies using randomly recruited, broadly representative participants, balanced evidence and accessible participation, with a required public government response and elected decision-making retained.

### The Problem

A well-designed assembly can examine evidence, hear competing views and explain trade-offs. Queensland should pilot assemblies for selected major decisions, preserving elected responsibility and ensuring the process has enough time, independence and accessibility to be worthwhile.

### What it costs

**Queensland estimate: not yet costed.** Cost recruitment, participant support, evidence preparation, facilitation, publication and evaluation. Compare with other consultation methods; no savings claim is assumed.

**Paid for by:** Proposed route — A separately identified government consultation and deliberation allocation. Funding is subject to appraisal and budget approval.

### What it does

- **Representative recruitment** — Use independently administered random selection with demographic balancing and transparent recruitment methods.
- **Define the remit** — Publish the remit, budget, evidence standards, decision timetable and how recommendations will be considered.
- **Balanced evidence** — Provide balanced expert and community evidence, opportunities to question it and independent facilitation.
- **Support participation** — Pay reasonable participation costs and support carers, interpreters, disability access and regional travel.
- **Respond publicly** — Require a published response to recommendations explaining adoption, modification or rejection, followed by independent evaluation.

### Research behind this policy

Queensland's Parliament has one elected chamber. An assembly would be an additional advisory process, not another chamber or an automatic power to enact legislation.

[Queensland Parliament: institutional structure](https://www.parliament.qld.gov.au/Visit-and-learn/About-Us)

<details>
<summary><strong>Further Detail</strong></summary>

---

**Design Rationale**

Open consultation tends to attract people already able and willing to participate. Random recruitment addresses a different selection problem, while balanced evidence and a public remit define what the assembly can consider. Representation should not be confused with a claim that recommendations bind every resident.

**System Interaction**

Queensland's elected Parliament retains legislative authority. The assembly advises within a specified remit and the government response accounts for adoption, modification or rejection. Recruitment support reduces participation barriers but non-response and attrition can still affect representativeness. Deliberation also requires time, creating a genuine tension with decisions that cannot reasonably wait; a selected-issue model preserves that boundary.

**Economic & Institutional Logic**

Representative deliberation requires recruitment, accessibility support and facilitation. Its institutional value should be assessed against other consultation methods and the time required for a decision.

**Risk & Failure Modes**

Protect participant privacy and avoid sponsor control. Assemblies advise within a public remit; Parliament retains legislative responsibility. Do not delay urgent decisions through an inflexible universal requirement.

**Evidence & Precedent**

Queensland's Parliament has one elected chamber. An assembly would be an additional advisory process, not another chamber or an automatic power to enact legislation. Queensland-specific evidence still required: Set recruitment design, decision criteria, response obligations and independent governance before selecting the first issue.

**Implementation Outline**

A provisional sequence for appraisal is set out in the [Delivery Plan](Fusion_Queensland_Delivery_Plan.md). First 6 months: develop governance and select a suitable pilot issue. Months 6-18: recruit and conduct the assembly, then publish recommendations and the response. Years 2-4: assess representativeness, influence and cost before committing to routine use. These stages are planning assumptions, not approved deadlines.

**References**

[Queensland Parliament: institutional structure](https://www.parliament.qld.gov.au/Visit-and-learn/About-Us)
